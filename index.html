The thesis

A name for the paperwork every project waits on.

Nothing in American infrastructure moves without a utility permit. A power line crossing a railroad, a gas main under a highway, a solar farm tying into a substation, a new housing tract connecting to water and power — each one needs a signed agreement from the owner of the ground, the wire, or the pipe it touches. The construction can be priced to the dollar. The permit cannot. It arrives when it arrives, and the whole project waits.

That wait has a price, and the price is printed on the page. CSX, the railroad, runs a dedicated Utility Permits page and charges $7,500 for expedited review — 15 working days to an agreement for signatures — on top of engineering review and license fees. Genesee & Wyoming, the short-line operator, quotes six to eight weeks for standard processing and $3,000 more to cut it to one or two weeks. Norfolk Southern's pipe-and-wire program charges $7,000 for an aerial wireline crossing, $8,000 underground, and $9,000 for a pipeline crossing. These are not filing fees for a form. They are the toll for crossing someone else's property with a utility, and every developer in the country pays them.

Behind the toll sits a whole trade. Permit expediters — firms whose only product is moving a file through a bureaucracy — charge $200 to $1,000 for a typical job, $500 to $5,000 for commercial work, and more than $15,000 for large or intricate projects, according to industry pricing guides. In New Jersey, ClearPath Permits starts at $349 per permit and handles PSE&G and Jersey Central Power & Light service-connection applications as a core line. In New York City, expediters quote $500 to $5,000 and up, with Department of Transportation permits at $500 to $2,000. The trade exists because the alternative — a contractor's own staff spending days on hold with a utility — costs more than the expediter does.

Exact, and built for a reachable buyer pool. The buyers are nameable: the permit-expediting firms in every major metro, the utility-coordination companies that manage crossings for developers, the electrical and solar contractors whose jobs cannot start without a service connection, and the developers themselves. They all search the same two words. This domain is those two words, in order, with nothing added.
