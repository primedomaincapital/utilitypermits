Two Thousand Gigawatts in Line

More than 2,000 gigawatts of generation and storage are waiting for permission to connect to the American grid. Most of it will never be built — and the queue itself decides which projects live.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

The panels are in the ground, the inverters are bolted to their pads, and the fence is up. From the county road, the solar farm looks finished. It is not finished. It has no permission to send a single watt into the grid, and the letter that grants that permission is somewhere in a queue with 8,200 other projects, behind studies that will take years and upgrades that will cost millions. The developer calls it "the interconnection queue." The crew that built the field calls it the waiting room.

The waiting room is the largest number in American energy. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's Queued Up 2026 report, which tracks the interconnection queues of more than 50 transmission operators covering about 98 percent of installed U.S. generating capacity, counted about 8,200 projects actively seeking grid connection at the end of 2025, representing 1,312 gigawatts of generation and approximately 749 gigawatts of storage — 2,061 gigawatts in total, roughly equal to all the generating capacity currently operating in the United States. The total fell 10 percent from the year before, not because the grid got faster but because withdrawals outnumbered new entries: more than 750 gigawatts left the queues in 2025. The mix is shifting under the surface. Active natural gas capacity rose 86 percent in 2025, to 253 gigawatts, while solar fell 19 percent to 773 gigawatts, storage fell 16 percent to 749 gigawatts, and wind fell 19 percent to 220 gigawatts. Another 549 gigawatts already holds a draft or executed interconnection agreement and still is not operating — 256 gigawatts of solar, 161 of storage, 76 of wind, and 45 of gas — projects that have their paper and are still waiting on construction, financing, or the network upgrades their agreements require.

Time is the part that compounds. For the projects that reached commercial operation in 2025, the median duration from interconnection request to operation was more than five years, in the regions where Berkeley Lab could measure it — up from about three years a decade earlier. In PJM, the grid operator for 13 states and the District of Columbia, the average interconnection timeline stretched from under two years in 2008 to roughly eight years by 2025. The outcome data is starker still: of the capacity that submitted interconnection requests between 2000 and 2020, only 13 percent had reached commercial operation by the end of 2025. Seventy-five percent had been withdrawn. The queue is not a pipeline. It is a trial, and most entrants fail it.

The federal regulator has spent three years trying to rewrite the trial. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission issued Order No. 2023 on July 28, 2023, replacing the old first-come, first-served serial study process with first-ready, first-served cluster studies: projects are grouped and studied together, under firmer site-control and deposit requirements, with a single 150-day cluster study, enforceable study deadlines backed by penalties of up to $2,500 per business day, and a deposit of 20 percent of network upgrade costs due when the interconnection agreement is executed. The rule took effect on November 6, 2023, and FERC issued Order No. 2023-A on March 21, 2024 to revise and clarify parts of it. The intent is plain: make queue positions cost real money to hold, so speculative projects leave and serious ones move. The early evidence — the 10 percent drop in total queue volume in 2025, driven by withdrawals — suggests the filter is working. Whether the serious projects move any faster is a question the next two years of data will answer.

The demand behind the queue is not in doubt. The United States installed a record 50 gigawatts of new solar capacity in 2024, according to the Solar Energy Industries Association and Wood Mackenzie, and solar accounted for 66 percent of all new generating capacity added to the grid that year. Developers planned to install 19.6 gigawatts of utility-scale battery storage in 2025, nearly double the 10.4 gigawatts installed in 2024, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration. The Edison Electric Institute reported that its member companies invested a record $178.2 billion in 2024 and are projected to invest more than $1.1 trillion between 2025 and 2029, with 91 gigawatts of new capacity under construction and 488 gigawatts planned or proposed. The power is being built. The question is which of it gets to connect, and when, and at whose expense — because every network upgrade the studies identify has to be paid for by someone, and the cluster process assigns that cost by proportional impact, so one project's withdrawal can raise the bill for every project left in its cluster.

The counterargument is that the queue is doing exactly what a queue should do. Most of the 2,061 gigawatts was never going to be built: developers file multiple requests for the same site, hold positions while they shop for financing, and withdraw when the numbers stop working. A queue that holds twice the country's generating capacity is not a backlog of real projects; it is an options book, and the withdrawals are the options expiring. On this view, the 13 percent completion rate is not a failure — it is the market sorting real projects from paper ones, and Order No. 2023's deposits and penalties are simply making the sorting faster and more honest. The gas surge in the queue supports the point: when buyers want firm capacity, developers file gas, and the queue reflects demand rather than blocking it.

There is force in that argument, and it still leaves the developer in the field with the same problem. Even if nine of ten queued projects deserve to die, the tenth — the one with financing, land, and panels in the ground — still waits more than five years for its letter, and still pays for studies and upgrades whose cost depends on strangers in its cluster. Interconnection position has become an asset in its own right: developers buy and sell queue positions, lenders price them, and the firms that can steer a project through the studies — the coordinators, the engineers, the permit specialists who know which grid operator wants what — are the ones the money follows. The grid is being rebuilt at a trillion-dollar pace, and every gigawatt of it passes through a permit. The companies that own that passage, and the name that describes it, are the ones this market rewards.
