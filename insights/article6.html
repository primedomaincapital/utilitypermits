The Permit Expediter, Priced: What the People Who Sell Time Actually Charge

Expediters charge $500 to $15,000 or more, flat or $40 to $300 an hour. The firms claim approvals 2.5 times faster; the independent evidence belongs to software. The real product is the avoidable delay.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The quote from the Los Angeles permit expediter arrived as a single page: $3,500 flat, plus city fees at cost, plus an hourly rate of $150 to $250 if the project hit trouble. The developer stared at the page and did the arithmetic that every developer does. The project was a restaurant buildout, and the rent on the empty space was $9,000 a month. If the expediter saved six weeks, the fee paid for itself three times over. If the expediter saved nothing, the $3,500 was a donation to a person who knew the clerks by name. The question was not whether $3,500 was a lot of money. It was whether time, in this building department, was actually for sale.

The price list, at least, is public. Ocean Permits & Development, a Los Angeles firm that published a 2026 cost guide, puts expediting fees in Los Angeles at $1,500 to $4,000 for a simple residential project such as an accessory dwelling unit or remodel, and $3,500 to $15,000 or more for commercial work — a restaurant buildout, a tenant improvement, new construction — with complex entitlements running to $25,000 or more. JDJ Consulting's California guide puts the range at $500 to $15,000 and above, with a small residential remodel at $500 to $2,000, a restaurant or retail job at $2,000 to $5,000, and a major development at $5,000 to $15,000 or more. In Houston, PermitFlow's guide puts expediters at $75 to $200 per hour, with flat fees from a few hundred dollars for a simple project to several thousand for a complex one. KD Architects, writing about the District of Columbia, Maryland, and Virginia market in October 2026, puts residential expediting at $500 to $1,500, commercial and complex projects at $2,500 to $8,000 or more, and hourly rates at $75 to $150. GBTimes, surveying the market in 2025, puts the range at $500 to $10,000 and above, with hourly rates of $75 to $250 or more. The spread is wide because the job is wide: a sign permit and a commercial entitlement are both called expediting, and they have nothing in common except the building department.

There are three ways to buy the service, and the model matters more than the number. The flat fee — $1,500 to $35,000 or more in Los Angeles, depending on scope — fits a defined job: an accessory dwelling unit, a standard tenant improvement, a restaurant buildout without entitlements. The client knows the price before filing, and the expediter owns the risk of a long review. The hourly rate — $150 to $250 an hour in Los Angeles, $40 to $300 an hour across California, $75 to $200 in Houston — fits the undefined job: a project mid-process, an entitlement hearing, a file that has already hit trouble. The hybrid — a base flat fee plus $150 to $250 an hour for additional scope — fits the job that looks defined and is not. Los Angeles adds a fourth charge that is not the expediter's at all: the Department of Building and Safety's Express plan-check fee, typically 50 to 80 percent of the standard plan-check fee as a premium to enter the priority queue. The expediter's fee buys the person. The Express fee buys the place in line. They are different purchases, and a quote that blends them is a quote that hides the difference.

Whether the purchase works is a harder question, because the evidence is mostly the sellers'. PermitFlow, the New York platform founded in 2021 by Francis Thumpasery and Samuel Lam, says its customers get approvals roughly 2.5 times faster and an estimated fivefold return on the fee, across more than $20 billion in construction value and 7,000 jurisdictions, with named customers including Lennar, Amazon, IKEA, and Toll Brothers — and it raised a $54 million Series B led by Accel in December 2025. The independent evidence is stronger for automation than for people: NREL's SolarAPP+ platform, which issues instant permits for code-compliant residential solar, finished projects about 14.5 business days sooner than traditional permitting in 2023, with a median 33-day permit-to-inspection timeline against 47.5 days for manual review. Houston reported permit times dropping from nine days to one after adopting SolarAPP+. Fresno issues eligible residential solar permits in real time through the platform. San Francisco, the cautionary case PermitFlow itself cites, can average close to 33 months for an approval. The numbers that are independently measured belong to software. The numbers that belong to human expediters belong to the firms selling them.

That gap points to what an expediter actually sells, which is not speed in the abstract. An expediter sells completeness, sequencing, and familiarity: the application filed with every exhibit attached, the corrections answered the day they arrive, the knowledge that this reviewer wants the fire detail before the structural detail. The delay an expediter can reliably prevent is the continuance — and the cost of a continuance is measurable. In Philadelphia, a missed exhibit or a bad posting is among the most common reasons a zoning hearing gets continued, usually by four to six months. In Los Angeles, a plan-check correction cycle runs weeks. What an expediter cannot sell is a shorter queue, a faster hearing calendar, or a study that the utility has not finished. The interconnection queue that holds 2,061 gigawatts does not move faster because someone knows the clerk. The environmental review that takes a median of 2.2 to 2.8 years does not shorten because the application was hand-delivered. The expediter's real product is the avoidable delay, and the avoidable delay is worth exactly what the project loses while it waits.

There is a case for hiring one on almost any commercial job, and it is arithmetic. A restaurant paying $9,000 a month in rent on an empty space recovers a $3,500 fee in less than two weeks of saved time. A developer carrying a construction loan recovers a $15,000 fee in a single month of avoided interest on a mid-size job. The fee is small against the carrying cost, and the carrying cost is the largest number in the project that nobody budgets. The counterweight is that the fee is not small against the government fee it sits beside: a Los Angeles accessory dwelling unit that costs $1,500 to $4,000 to expedite may cost less than that in city fees, and a simple residential permit that the SolarAPP+ platform issues instantly costs the applicant nothing in expediting at all. Paying a person to move a file that software moves for free is not a bargain. It is a habit. And a flat fee on a job that turns out to be undefined is the worst of both models: the price was fixed, the scope was not, and the hourly meter starts when the flat fee runs out.

The way to buy the service, then, is to buy it for what it can actually do. Hire for completeness on a defined scope, and pay a flat fee — with the fee, the jurisdiction's fees at cost, and any engineering listed separately, in writing, before anything is filed. Hire hourly only for trouble: a file already in process, an entitlement hearing, a project the city has already bounced. Measure the expediter on continuances avoided and correction cycles closed, not on days promised, because the days are mostly not theirs to promise. And before signing, ask the question the quote is designed to skip: which delay, exactly, does this fee remove. If the answer is the queue, the hearing calendar, or the utility study, keep the money. If the answer is the missing exhibit, the wrong form, and the four-month continuance that follows them, pay it. Time is for sale in the building department. Just make sure you are buying time, and not a tour of the waiting room.
