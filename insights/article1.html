The Paperwork Behind the Power Lines

A solar farm can be built in months. Its utility permit can take years. The bottleneck is not steel or silicon — it is a signature.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

The plans were finished in March. The trench was dug in April, the conduit laid in May, and by June the crew had gone home. The pipe sat in the ground all summer, capped at both ends, waiting on a single piece of paper: a railroad's permission for a utility line to cross its tracks. The developer had budgeted for steel, for labor, for the pipe itself. Nobody had budgeted for the six months the pipe would spend doing nothing, because nobody on the project had ever priced a wait.

Every utility line in America crosses something that belongs to someone else — a railroad right-of-way, a highway, a river, another utility's easement — and each crossing needs a signed agreement before work begins. The railroads, who own some of the most crossed ground in the country, have turned that agreement into a published price list. CSX runs a dedicated Utility Permits page and charges $7,500 for expedited review, which buys an agreement for signatures within 15 working days, on top of the engineering review fee and license fees. Genesee & Wyoming, which operates short lines across the country, quotes six to eight weeks for standard processing and $3,000 more to cut that to one or two weeks; its published fee schedule lists a $1,000 application fee, a $2,250 engineering review fee for a standard utility installation, and a $1,750 right-of-entry fee. Norfolk Southern's pipe-and-wire program, run through RailPros, charges $7,000 for an aerial wireline crossing, $8,000 for an underground wireline crossing, and $9,000 for a pipeline crossing, with a $3,000 supplement fee and a $500 charge for each review after the first two. The numbers are not hidden. They are the toll, printed in the open.

The toll exists because the crossing is not a formality. A railroad that signs a utility agreement takes on a permanent neighbor inside its right-of-way: a pipe that can leak, a wire that can fall, a crew that will need access for repairs at 2 a.m. The engineering review checks clearances, depths, and casing; the license sets the rent; the flagging schedule protects the trains. The fees pay for engineers who read the plans and lawyers who write the agreement, and the expedited fee pays for jumping the queue — the railroad's own staff, pulled off other files. For the developer, the choice is arithmetic: $7,500 against the carrying cost of a stalled project, which on a mid-size job runs to thousands of dollars a week in interest, crew standby, and missed revenue. Most developers pay it, and pay it gladly.

The same arithmetic runs at a much larger scale across the whole grid. The Edison Electric Institute reported that its member companies — the investor-owned electric companies — invested a record $178.2 billion in 2024, the 13th consecutive year of record-setting investment, and are projected to invest more than $1.1 trillion between 2025 and 2029. The institute's October 2025 figures put 2025 spending alone at nearly $208 billion, with 91 gigawatts of new capacity under construction and 488 gigawatts planned or proposed. Every dollar of that spending becomes a project that needs permits: transmission lines crossing railroads and highways, substations tying into existing wires, service connections for the data centers and factories driving the demand. The U.S. Census Bureau counted 1,431,616 new privately owned housing units authorized by building permits in 2025, with a total valuation of $378.99 billion — down 3.1 percent from 1,478,000 units in 2024, but still 1.43 million new homes, each one needing a utility connection. The permit is not a side process in this build-out. It is the gate every project passes through, and the gate is getting busier.

Solar shows the same pattern from the other side. The United States installed a record 50 gigawatts of new solar capacity in 2024, according to the Solar Energy Industries Association and Wood Mackenzie — the largest single year of new capacity added by any energy technology in more than two decades, and 66 percent of all new generating capacity added to the grid that year. The utility-scale segment alone installed a record 41.4 gigawatts, up 33 percent from 2023, with Texas adding 11.6 gigawatts and 21 states setting annual installation records. Each of those projects needed interconnection agreements, easements, and utility permits before a single panel could feed the grid. The panels are the fast part. The paperwork is the slow part, and the gap between the two is where projects die.

The counterargument is that the fees and the waits are the system working as designed. A railroad that rubber-stamped crossings would be gambling with derailments; a utility that connected every applicant on the day they asked would be gambling with grid stability. The published fee schedules are, in this telling, a sign of health: transparent prices, published timelines, and an expedited lane for those willing to pay. Portals have replaced paper in many jurisdictions, and some states now cap review times by statute. The bottleneck, on this view, is a filter that keeps bad projects out and forces good ones to arrive with complete plans.

There is truth in that, and it does not change the conclusion. A filter that takes six to eight weeks — or five years, at the grid-connection end of the business — is still a filter that decides which projects get built and which do not, and the firms that know how to move through it are the ones that get paid. Developers now budget the permit the way they budget steel: as a line item with a price, a timeline, and a person responsible for it. The companies that sell that competence — the expediters, the coordinators, the contractors who have done the crossing before — are selling the one thing a project schedule cannot manufacture: time. And the name that describes that competence, in the exact words every developer types into a search box, is the name on this page.
