How Long a Utility Permit Takes — and Which Approval Is the Long Pole

A home solar system clears interconnection in 25 to 64 days. A new grid connection took a median of 61 months in 2025. The long pole is the queue — and it is not the local permit.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The panels were in the warehouse. The racking was on site. The crew had been hired, the loan had been drawn, and the project was ready to build — except for one signature, which belonged to a queue. The developer had applied for grid interconnection three years earlier. The median new grid connection in 2025 took 61 months from request to power flowing. The project was not behind schedule. It was on the schedule the grid sets, and the grid's schedule is measured in years while a construction schedule is measured in weeks.

The queue is the long pole, and its size is the first thing to grasp. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's "Queued Up: 2026 Edition" counted about 2,061 gigawatts of generation and storage waiting for grid connection at the end of 2025 — more than the roughly 1,374 gigawatts of capacity installed across the entire American grid. The median project waits more than three years just to sign an interconnection agreement, and more than five years to reach commercial operation. In PJM, the grid operator covering much of the Mid-Atlantic and Midwest, average timelines have stretched toward eight years. The median time for a new grid connection was 61 months in 2025, up from 22 months in 2008. Of the projects that requested a connection between 2000 and 2020, only about one in five had reached commercial operation by the end of 2025, and of those that applied between 2000 and 2019, 77 percent withdrew while 13 percent went live. The queue is not a line that moves. It is a warehouse where most of what enters never leaves as a power plant.

The study clock inside the queue is the part the rules now govern. Under the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission's Order No. 2023, issued July 2023, transmission providers run an annual cluster process: a 45-day window for interconnection requests, a 60-day customer engagement window, then 150 days for the initial cluster study and 150 days for any restudy. Providers that miss the deadlines pay $1,000 per business day for a late cluster study, $2,000 for a late restudy or affected-system study, and $2,500 for a late facilities study, capped at 100 percent of the study deposits in the cluster, with the penalties refunded to affected customers on a pro rata basis — a structure the D.C. Circuit confirmed when it upheld the order in July 2026. The deadlines are real, and the queue did shrink about 10 percent from 2024 to 2025. But the deadlines govern studies, not connections, and the operators with the worst backlogs found other ways to stop the clock: PJM paused review of new requests from 2022 until 2026, and CAISO accepted no new interconnection requests in 2024 or 2025. A 150-day study deadline means little when the operator is not accepting requests at all. The reform capped the study. It did not cap the wait.

The environmental clock runs beside the queue, and for projects that need federal approval it can be just as long. The most recent government study put the median time to complete an environmental impact statement at 2.8 years, and for final impact statements issued in 2024 the median was 2.2 years from notice of intent to final document. Environmental assessments completed by the Department of Transportation between 2021 and 2023 averaged 9.6 months. The Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023 imposed hard caps — two years for an impact statement, one year for an assessment — and Congress added a pay-for-speed option: an applicant may pay 125 percent of the cost of preparing the documents for a 180-day assessment deadline and a one-year impact statement deadline. The Supreme Court weighed in during 2025 in Seven County Infrastructure Coalition v. Eagle County, Colorado, narrowing the scope of what an environmental review must consider. Historically, about 16 percent of impact statements and less than 1 percent of all National Environmental Policy Act actions have faced court challenges. The environmental review is not usually where a project dies. It is where a project spends two years finding out whether it lives.

The contrast with the small-project clock is what makes the long pole visible. A residential solar system clears interconnection in 25 to 64 days nationally, according to NREL's SolarTRACE data from 2022. PPL Electric in Pennsylvania answers most residential applications under 25 kilowatts within three to four weeks. Pacific Gas and Electric takes 10 to 20 business days for review, plus up to 95 more business days if a detailed engineering study is triggered. SolarAPP+, the automated permitting platform built with NREL, cut the median permit-to-inspection timeline to 33 days against 47.5 days for manual review, and projects using it finished about 14.5 business days sooner. Florida's HB 683, effective July 1, 2025, requires single-trade residential permits, including solar, to be approved within five business days or be automatically approved. The small job is measured in days. The large job is measured in years. Storage-paired systems run slower still: permission to operate averages 8 to 12 weeks against 4 to 6 weeks for solar-only installs, with complex jobs stretching to 16 weeks, according to 2026 trade data reported by Eos Loan. They are not the same process with different durations. They are different processes that happen to share a name.

There is a case for the queue, and it is stronger than developers admit. Most queued projects would never be built even without delay — the 77 percent withdrawal rate says the filter is catching speculation, not just slowing construction. A grid operator that connected every applicant on the day they asked would be gambling with grid stability, and the cluster-study process at least studies projects together, so one project's upgrades do not strand the next. The queue shrank 10 percent in a year, which is the first sign in a decade that the reforms are moving the number. The counterweight is that the filter charges its price in years of a serious developer's life, and the years are not recoverable: a project that waits five years for a connection agreement has spent five years of land options, engineering, and financing commitments on a signature. The queue is a filter. It is also a tax, paid in time, by the projects it was built to serve.

The implication is to treat the interconnection queue as the project, not as a step in it. A transmission-scale developer should budget five years from request to operation, assume the study deadlines govern the paperwork and not the connection, and site the project where the queue is shortest rather than where the sun is strongest — the regional numbers in the Berkeley Lab report run from 567 gigawatts in the West to 408 gigawatts in ERCOT, and those are different waits. A mid-size developer should ask whether the project can connect at the distribution level, where the clock is measured in weeks, instead of the transmission level, where it is measured in years. And anyone financing a project should write the interconnection timeline into the loan documents before the loan is drawn, because the lender's clock and the queue's clock are the two schedules that decide whether the project gets built. The panels are the fast part. The permission is the project.
