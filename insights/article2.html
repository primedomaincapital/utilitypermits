The People Who Sell Time

Permit expediters charge a few hundred dollars to move a file from the bottom of a pile to the top. The business is small, unglamorous, and paid for by the most expensive thing on any job: waiting.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

At 7:45 on a Tuesday morning, the line outside the county permitting office is already ten deep, and the woman at the front is not a homeowner. She is a permit runner, and the stack of folders under her arm belongs to five different contractors, none of whom is here. She knows the clerk by name, knows which reviewer handles electrical, knows that the plans for the third folder will come back with a comment about the service panel schedule, and has already fixed it once. She will be back on Thursday with the corrected set. The contractors pay her so they never have to stand in this line at all.

That is the permit-expediting business in one scene: a person who knows a building department the way a regular knows a diner, selling the hours a contractor would otherwise lose. The prices are published, and they are modest next to the jobs they serve. CostOwl's pricing guide puts a permit expeditor at $200 to $1,000 on average, with minor residential work — an electrical or plumbing permit — at $200 to $400, and commercial projects at $800 to $5,000 and up. The filing fees themselves run $100 to $500 per permit for residential work and $300 to $1,000 and up for commercial, with rush processing at $100 to $500 and revisions at $50 to $200 each. Nationwide Permit's commercial guide puts expediting at $500 to $5,000 per permit, with hourly rates of $75 to $150, and fees above $15,000 for larger or more intricate projects; on the West Coast, where California Title 24 and seismic rules apply, the same guide puts typical fees at $2,000 to $8,000.

The firms have organized themselves around the same price list, city by city. In New Jersey, ClearPath Permits charges from $349 for a single permit, from $599 for a full bundle of construction, electrical, and fire subcodes, and from $1,095 a month for a retainer — and its core line is not the building permit at all but the utility forms: PSE&G and Jersey Central Power & Light service-connection and upgrade applications, prepared within 24 hours, across all 21 New Jersey counties. In Florida, Best Permit Runners lists an electrical permit at $300, a plumbing permit at $300, a small building permit at $450, and a large one from $1,200, with each additional sub-permit at $250 and inspections through close-out at $200. In New York City, PermitExpertsNYC quotes $500 to $5,000 and up depending on the permit, with Department of Transportation permits at $500 to $2,000 and city filing fees of $280 to $2,100 on top. TaskHatch, a newer managed-filing service, prices permit-only filing at $150 and filing plus inspection coordination at $250, with inspection coordination as a $100 add-on and resubmittals at $75. The spread is wide, but the shape is the same everywhere: a flat fee per permit, a premium for speed, and a retainer for the contractor who files every week.

The volume behind those fees is enormous. The U.S. Census Bureau counted 1,431,616 new privately owned housing units authorized by building permits in 2025, with a total valuation of $378.99 billion. Each one of those units needed permits, and a large share of the commercial work behind them — the offices, the warehouses, the solar arrays — needed utility permits on top. An expediter who handles 20 permits a month at $349 each takes in about $7,000 a month from that one line; a firm with five runners and a retainer book is a real business, with real recurring revenue, built on a service that cannot be offshored and cannot be done by a homeowner with a weekend to spare.

What the expediter actually sells is not the filing. Anyone can file. What the expediter sells is the second and third trip: the reviewer comment answered the same day instead of the same month, the missing form spotted before submission instead of after rejection, the phone number of the person who can say yes. A first-time applicant files, waits three weeks, gets a comment, fixes it, waits three more weeks, and discovers a second comment. An expediter files once, correctly, because the same reviewer has sent the same comment to the same firm a hundred times. The fee is a few hundred dollars. The saving is measured in weeks of a project schedule, and on a commercial job a week is worth far more than the fee.

The counterargument is that the trade is shrinking from both ends. Cities have moved permitting online — Chicago's ePlan, San Antonio's portal, Accela systems in county after county — and an online portal does not care whether the person uploading the plans is a contractor or an expediter. Some jurisdictions now issue over-the-counter permits for simple work the same day. Software firms are building filing tools that check a plan set against the code before it is submitted, and TaskHatch's $150 permit-only price shows how far the floor has fallen for a straight filing. If the whole business is knowing which form goes where, a good portal and a good checklist should, in time, replace it.

That argument mistakes the filing for the work. Portals standardize the upload; they do not standardize the reviewer, the utility, or the railroad, and they do not answer the phone. The expediter's durable edge is not the form — it is the relationship with the office that reads the form, the memory of what that office asked for last time, and the willingness to stand in line on a Tuesday morning so a contractor can be on a job site instead. The firms that survive will be the ones that pair the portal with the person: software for the routine, a runner for the exception. And the name that tells a contractor, in two words, exactly what that firm does — the name a developer types when the project is stalled and the clock is running — is the name that wins the call.
