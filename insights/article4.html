What a Utility Permit Costs: Fees, Studies, and the Price of the Queue

A home solar permit costs a few hundred dollars. A utility-scale project pays a $5,000 application fee, a study deposit up to $250,000, and an environmental review that averages $6 million.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The budget meeting for the five-megawatt solar project had a line for panels, a line for racking, a line for labor — and then a line nobody in the room could explain. It said "interconnection deposit: $110,000." The developer's finance director asked what the deposit bought. The answer was that it bought a place in line, and that the money was only partly refundable if the project walked away. The panels had a price list. The permit had a fee schedule. The queue had a toll, and the toll was the largest number on the page that was not a piece of equipment.

At the small end of the market, the numbers are modest and mostly printed. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory's 2023 benchmarking study found that permitting, inspection, and interconnection average 7.5 percent of total installed residential solar cost — about $0.42 per watt against a median installed price of $5.60 per watt. NREL's analysis of 500 jurisdictions found permit costs ranging from $150 in Phoenix, Arizona, and Reno, Nevada, through the SolarAPP+ platform, to $2,200 in San Francisco for a full historic-overlay review. The utility's own fee is usually the smallest piece: PPL Electric in Pennsylvania charges $100 for a certified inverter-based system of 10 kilowatts or less, $250 plus $1 per kilowatt for systems up to 2,000 kilowatts, and $350 plus $2 per kilowatt for larger or non-inverter-based systems. Pacific Gas and Electric charges $145 for a residential interconnection application; Consolidated Edison charges nothing for systems under 25 kilowatts; Xcel Energy in Minnesota charges $100; Duke Energy Florida charges $400 for the bidirectional meter swap. Orange County, California, charges $448 for a roof-mounted residential solar permit in fiscal year 2025-26, $890 for a ground-mounted system, and $939 for a commercial system — and California's AB 1414 caps residential permit fees at $450 for systems up to 15 kilowatts. Modesto charges $333 for a residential photovoltaic permit, while the Modesto Irrigation District charges $900 for interconnection below 100 kilowatts. In Midvale, Utah, an 8-kilowatt system priced at $18,800 carries about $384 in city and utility fees, including Rocky Mountain Power's $60 interconnection charge. The residential permit is a few hundred dollars. It is the last time in this business that anything costs a few hundred dollars.

At utility scale, the price structure changes completely, and the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission wrote the new rules in Order No. 2023, issued July 28, 2023. An interconnection customer now pays a non-refundable $5,000 application fee to enter the queue, plus a single initial study deposit that runs from $55,000 to $250,000 depending on project size. Then comes the commercial readiness deposit: twice the study deposit at the first study stage, then 5 percent of assigned network upgrade costs at the cluster restudy and 10 percent at the facilities study. A customer that withdraws after executing a cluster study agreement faces withdrawal penalties tied to those upgrade costs. The order also set firm study deadlines — 150 days for an initial cluster study and 150 days for a restudy — with penalties of $1,000 per business day for a late cluster study, $2,000 for a late restudy or affected-system study, and $2,500 for a late facilities study. On July 31, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit upheld Order No. 2023 in its entirety in Advanced Energy United v. FERC, preserving the cluster-study process, the readiness deposits, and the penalty schedule. The deposits are not fees for a service rendered. They are a filter, priced to keep speculative projects out of the queue — and priced high enough that a developer who enters the queue has already committed real capital to the idea of building.

The environmental study is the third price, and it is the one that scales least predictably. A Phase I environmental site assessment typically costs $2,000 to $6,000; a Phase II investigation runs from a few thousand dollars to six figures depending on contamination and site size. Under the National Environmental Policy Act, the federal review itself is far larger: a categorical exclusion runs about $50,000, an environmental assessment about $200,000 in Forest Service data, and an environmental impact statement averages $6 million at the Department of Energy, with complex statements reaching tens of millions of dollars. Congress added a pay-for-speed option in the Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023: an applicant may pay the agency 125 percent of the estimated cost of preparing the environmental documents in exchange for a 180-day deadline on an assessment and a one-year deadline on an impact statement. Paying a premium to buy a deadline is a strange transaction, and it tells you what the market thinks the delay is worth. The study is not a formality that happens to cost money. For a large project, it is a second project, with its own budget, its own consultants, and its own multi-year schedule.

The useful distinction in all of this is between the costs that scale with the project and the ones that do not. Per-kilowatt interconnection fees scale: PPL's $1 or $2 per kilowatt is trivial on a house and material on a megawatt. Study deposits scale by size band. Network upgrade costs scale with what the grid needs, which is why the readiness deposits are percentages of them. But the application fee is flat — $5,000 whether the project is 5 megawatts or 500. The environmental assessment is flat-ish by comparison too: a $200,000 assessment does not double because the project doubles, it grows because the site is harder. And the railroad crossing fees from this site's first article — CSX charging $7,500 for expedited review, Genesee & Wyoming $1,000 to apply plus $2,250 for engineering review — do not scale at all. A crossing is a crossing. The result is that small projects pay mostly flat fees, large projects pay mostly scaled deposits, and mid-size projects — the five-megawatt solar farm in the budget meeting — pay both, which is why their permitting line is the hardest to forecast.

There is a defense of the pricing, and the queue's own numbers support part of it. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory counted about 2,061 gigawatts of generation and storage waiting for grid connection at the end of 2025 — more capacity than the entire installed grid — and of the projects that requested a connection between 2000 and 2020, only about one in five had reached commercial operation by the end of 2025. A queue that big, filled with projects that mostly never build, is a queue that needs a filter, and deposits are the filter that works. The queue did shrink about 10 percent from 2024 to 2025 as the Order No. 2023 reforms took hold. The counterweight is that the filter charges the serious developer the same as the speculator at the door, and the serious developer is the one who pays the withdrawal penalty when a network upgrade estimate doubles between studies. The price of the queue is not only the deposit. It is the capital tied up in deposits for projects that may never clear the studies they paid for.

The implication for anyone budgeting a utility project is to price the permit as capital at risk, not as an expense. The residential installer can quote the fee to the dollar, because the fee schedule is printed and the utility's charge is $100 or $145 or $400. The utility-scale developer cannot, because the study deposit is a floor, the readiness deposit is a multiple, and the network upgrade — the number the percentages attach to — is not known until the study is done. Budget the $5,000 application fee and the $55,000 to $250,000 study deposit as money that may not come back. Budget the environmental review as its own project. And before entering a transmission queue, ask the question the deposit is designed to force: is this project ready enough that tying up six figures in a place in line is the best use of the money. If the answer is no, the cheapest permit is the one never applied for.
