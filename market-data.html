Utility permits, interconnection queues, and grid investment, by the numbers

Interconnection queue volumes, railroad utility-permit fees, permit-expediting prices, utility capital spending, and solar installation figures — from published sources.

Three different kinds of number sit behind this name, and they must not be mixed. The first kind measures the queue: the gigawatts waiting for permission to connect, which decide how long a project waits. The second kind measures the toll: the published fees railroads and expediters charge to move a permit, which decide what the wait costs. The third kind measures the build: the trillions utilities plan to spend and the gigawatts of solar going in, which decide how much work there is to permit. The first kind tells a reader how long the line is. The second tells a reader what it costs to move through it. The third tells a reader how fast the line is growing.

What the latest numbers show is a market where the work is growing faster than the permission: 2,061 gigawatts sat in interconnection queues at the end of 2025, the median project built that year took more than five years from request to operation, and utilities are projected to spend more than $1.1 trillion between 2025 and 2029 — while the railroads publish four-figure fees for a single crossing and expediters charge a few hundred dollars to move one file.

How to read these numbers

Included: Berkeley Lab's Queued Up interconnection queue figures through the end of 2025, with the end-of-2024 snapshot for comparison; FERC Order No. 2023 reform terms; published railroad utility-permit fees and processing times from CSX, Genesee & Wyoming, and Norfolk Southern / RailPros; published permit-expediting price guides from CostOwl, Nationwide Permit, ClearPath Permits, and PermitExpertsNYC; Edison Electric Institute capital-investment figures for 2024 and the 2025–2029 projection; SEIA / Wood Mackenzie 2024 solar installation figures and the EIA battery-storage plan for 2025; and the Census Bureau's 2025 building-permit totals. Excluded: any claim about what a single permit costs in a jurisdiction not listed above, since fees are set per railroad, per utility, and per jurisdiction; any queue figure presented as capacity that will be built, since only 13 percent of the 2000–2020 cohort reached operation; and any expediter rate presented as a quote, since published prices are starting points that vary by project.

Queue capacity is proposed capacity seeking connection, not capacity that will be built — Berkeley Lab's own data shows three-quarters of the 2000–2020 cohort withdrew. Railroad fees are the published schedule for the named crossing types; license and rental charges are additional. Expediting prices are published starting fees; the total cost of a permit includes the jurisdiction's own filing fees, passed through at cost. Forecasts are labeled as forecasts, not actuals. The 2024 and 2025 queue totals are different year-end snapshots from the same series and should not be combined into a single trend beyond the year-over-year change Berkeley Lab reports.
